# Coupons, BOGOs, and Stacking Rules Shoppers Misunderstand
**Dateline:** San Jose — September 2026
**Word count:** 704

*A deal board is not a blank check—ask what stacks, what is pre-tax, and what the register will actually honor.*

San Jose’s capped cannabis market leans hard on promotions: percentage-off days, BOGOs, loyalty points, and brand demos. Shoppers who treat every poster as stackable math often leave confused. The fix is not cynicism; it is asking four plain questions before you build a cart.

First: does the discount apply to the menu price before tax? California cannabis sales still collect sales tax under CDTFA rules, and San Jose’s combined sales and use tax rate has been 10.000 percent since April 1, 2026. A “20 percent off” sign almost always means 20 percent off the pre-tax shelf price, not off the final out-the-door total. If you mentally discount a tax-inclusive number, you will invent a bargain the register cannot match.

Second: what is excluded? Many BOGOs exclude concentrates, cartridges, or already reduced house brands. Some percentage-off days exclude the newest drops. Loyalty points may not apply to the same SKUs as a manufacturer coupon. None of that is unique to cannabis—grocery stores play the same game—but cannabis menus change quickly, so exclusions matter more.

Third: can offers stack? Stacking is a shop policy, not a state right. One retailer may allow a loyalty discount on top of a brand day; another may choose the greater of two offers. City registration and state licensing do not require shops to combine every flyer in the window. Ask before you assume. If an online menu shows a different price than the in-store board, ask which system is authoritative at checkout.

Fourth: is the promotion limited by daily purchase caps? A BOGO that would push you past adult-use daily limits—28.5 grams of non-concentrated cannabis, 8 grams of concentrate including product-contained concentrate, and 6 immature plants under California Code of Regulations title 4, section 15004.9—cannot be completed as advertised. Staff who split a deal across days or refuse part of a basket are applying state rules, not killing the vibe.

San Jose shoppers should also separate licensed promotions from social-media “menus” that are not tied to SJPD-registered businesses. A flashy BOGO from an unregistered seller is not a bargain; it is a compliance red flag. Verify the retailer on SJPD’s registered list before you chase a deal.

Medical donation pathways under SB 34 are not coupons. Compassion inventory, where available, follows physician-recommendation rules and limited supply. Do not pressure staff to redirect patient donations into recreational bargain hunting.

Practical habits help. Screenshot or photograph the deal board when you enter. Ask whether online carts honor the same stack. Keep receipts if a brand rebate requires proof of purchase. Compare unit prices—price per milligram THC on edibles, price per gram on flower—rather than only chasing the loudest percentage.

Promotions are how a scarce storefront market competes when many shelves carry overlapping brands. Used carefully, they save money inside the legal system. Used as an excuse to skip registration checks or ignore purchase limits, they become expensive mistakes. The budtender can explain stacking; only you can decide whether the out-the-door total still beats waiting for a simpler sale day.

Adults who already prefer licensed San Jose retailers can treat the guidance above as maintenance, not conversion: verify the shop, read the label, budget for tax, and leave gray-market shortcuts alone. That steady habit is how a capped local market stays accountable to the people who shop it.

When in doubt, reopen SJPD’s registered cannabis businesses page and the Department of Cannabis Control’s consumer “what’s legal” guide before you spend cash. Those two bookmarks answer more first-timer questions than any parking-lot rumor.
