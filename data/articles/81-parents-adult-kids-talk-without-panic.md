# Parents of Adult Kids: How to Talk About Cannabis Without Panic

*When a 22-year-old in San Jose shops legally, parents still have questions. Curiosity beats catastrophe scripts—and boundaries still matter.*

San Jose parents who raised kids through DARE-era messaging now watch those same kids—adults 21 and older—walk into registered dispensaries with state licenses and city paperwork. The cultural whiplash is real. Panic helps less than a structured conversation.

Start by updating your facts. California allows adults 21 and older to purchase and possess limited amounts of cannabis and to consume on private property under state rules, subject to local limits and employer policies. San Jose requires retailers to appear on SJPD’s registered list. Legal products are tested and labeled in ways parking-lot bags never were. That does not make cannabis risk-free; it makes the supply chain more inspectable.

Ask questions that assume adulthood. What formats are you using? How do you decide dose? Where do you store products when younger siblings or visiting cousins are around? Are you driving after consumption? Are you on a job path that still drug-tests? Questions beat monologues about 1970s scare films. Listen to the answers before you lecture.

Share concerns without catastrophizing. Edible over-intoxication is common among new users who redose too early—DCC advises patience because effects can take hours. Mental-health vulnerability is a fair topic if psychosis risk runs in the family; that is a clinician conversation, not a Facebook meme. Respiratory irritation from smoke is real; vapor and edibles change exposure profiles without erasing all risk. Pregnancy and nursing guidance from DCC is clear: do not consume, talk to a doctor.

Boundaries remain legitimate even when use is legal. You can refuse cannabis use inside your house. You can forbid products around minor relatives. You can decline to store someone else’s stash. Legal adult use does not obligate parents to host a smoke session in the garage. State it calmly and consistently.

Money conversations help. If you still subsidize rent or tuition, you can ask how cannabis spending fits a budget without demanding abstinence as theater. If you do not subsidize anything, your leverage is relational, not financial—use it sparingly and honestly.

If use looks compulsive, coupled with failing obligations, or entangled with other substance problems, shift from debate to care. Offer help finding clinical resources. Do not diagnose via group chat. Do not raid rooms of a rent-paying adult without understanding legal and relational consequences; focus on safety for minors in the home first.

Parents who use cannabis themselves should model the standards they preach: locked storage, no driving impaired, no use around kids, registered retailers only. Hypocrisy teaches faster than pamphlets.

The goal is not to win. The goal is fewer secrets, fewer dangerous myths, and clearer household rules. Adult kids in San Jose can shop legally; families can still talk like people who want each other alive, employed, and kind at Sunday dinner.

Local shoppers can keep this guidance beside the other essentials of adult-use life in San Jose: verify sellers on SJPD’s registered cannabis businesses list, keep receipts, store products away from children and pets, and consume only on private property where household rules allow. Those habits will not settle every debate, but they keep ordinary weekends inside the lawful, lower-drama lane this market was built to support.
