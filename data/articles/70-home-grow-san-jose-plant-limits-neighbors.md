*State law caps personal grows at six living plants per residence, with local rules layered on top. Odor fights are where many South Bay home grows actually break.*

California adults 21 and older may cultivate up to six living cannabis plants at a single private residence for personal use, with harvested amounts kept secured and not visible from a public place under Health and Safety Code rules summarized by the Department of Cannabis Control. That statewide ceiling is the starting point—not a promise that every San Jose balcony can become a greenhouse.

Local governments retain authority to regulate personal cultivation, including requiring indoor grows and restricting outdoor plants. Unincorporated Santa Clara County materials, for example, have emphasized indoor-only personal cultivation and nuisance standards around odor, light, and visibility. City of San Jose renters and HOA residents face additional private restrictions in leases and covenants. Before you buy soil, read your lease, your HOA rules, and local code—not only a statewide blog post.

Six plants is a household limit, not a per-person multiplier. Roommates cannot each claim six plants in the same residence under the standard adult-use framework DCC describes. Medical patients may have different physician-recommended allowances; that is a clinical and legal lane separate from casual adult-use gardening. Volatile solvent extraction at home is a hard no in consumer guidance—leave concentrates to licensed manufacturers.

Neighbor conflict usually begins with smell, not plant counts. Flowering cannabis can broadcast odor across fences, especially in still evening air. Even a lawful indoor tent can annoy adjoining units if filtration is weak or exhaust vents toward a shared light well. Treat odor control as mandatory hospitality: carbon filters, sealed tents, sensible exhaust routing, and honesty if a neighbor complains. Escalating with “it’s legal” rarely de-escalates a smell complaint. Nuisance standards can still apply when odors interfere with others’ reasonable enjoyment of property.

Visibility and security are the other tripwires. Plants and product above possession thresholds must be locked and not visible by normal unaided vision from a public place under state personal-cultivation rules. Street-facing garage grows with open doors invite theft and enforcement attention. Keep drying racks private. Do not advertise your harvest on the neighborhood app.

Renters should assume “no” until written permission says otherwise. Landlords worry about mold from humidity, electrical loads from lights, and insurance. A stealth tent that trips a breaker or stains a ceiling can cost more than years of dispensary flower. If you cannot get written approval, licensed retail remains the cleaner path—especially in a city where registered storefronts already exist for adults who want tested product without horticultural drama.

Home growing appeals to hobbyists, privacy seekers, and people frustrated by tax-heavy shelf prices. It also demands electrical safety, fire awareness, water discipline, and neighbor diplomacy. San Jose’s dense parcels leave little buffer between your hobby and someone else’s bedroom window. Stay at or under six plants, follow local and lease rules, invest in odor control, and remember that a peaceful block is part of keeping personal cultivation politically and practically viable.

If a dispute starts, document your compliance, improve filtration, and talk before anyone calls code enforcement. The goal is not to win a forum argument about Prop 64. The goal is to keep a lawful hobby from becoming a building-wide grievance.

Shoppers who decide home growing is not worth the neighbor risk are not failing at cannabis culture. They are choosing the licensed aisle that San Jose already regulates with security cameras, testing, and tax collection. Hobby cultivation remains lawful within limits for many adults, but it is optional. If your building, schedule, or relationships cannot support a clean grow, buy tested flower and spend your weekends on something that does not perfume the light well.

Local shoppers can keep this guidance next to the other boring essentials of adult-use life in San Jose: check SJPD’s registered cannabis list before trying a new seller, keep receipts, store products away from children and pets, and consume only on private property where household rules allow. Those habits will not settle every debate, but they keep ordinary weekends inside the lawful, lower-drama lane this market was built to support.
