# Adult-Use vs. Medical Card: What Changes at the San Jose Counter

*Age gates, daily purchase limits, and paperwork differ—public consumption rules mostly do not.*

California lets adults buy cannabis two related ways: adult-use sales for customers 21 and older, and medicinal sales for patients 18 and older who hold a physician’s recommendation. San Jose’s registered retailers commonly serve both lanes under dual licensing. Knowing which lane you are in changes the ID check and the daily limits—not the city’s ban on lighting up in Plaza de César Chávez.

The Department of Cannabis Control’s consumer guidance states the age rules plainly. You can buy cannabis if you are 21 or older for adult use, or 18 or older with a physician’s recommendation for medicinal use. Retailers must verify identity and age. Adult-use customers should expect a government ID showing they are at least 21. Medicinal customers should expect the retailer to review a valid physician’s recommendation or medical identification documentation consistent with state rules, plus proof of identity. Shops may refuse a sale when paperwork is incomplete or expired; that is compliance, not personal judgment.

Daily purchase limits differ by lane under California Code of Regulations title 4, section 15004.9 and related Health and Safety Code provisions. For a single adult-use customer in a single day, licensed retailers may not sell more than 28.5 grams of non-concentrated cannabis, 8 grams of cannabis concentrate (including concentrate contained in cannabis products), and 6 immature plants. For a medicinal patient—or a primary caregiver buying on the patient’s behalf—the daily retail limits are higher on flower: up to 8 ounces of medicinal cannabis in dried mature flower form (or plant conversion as provided in HSC 11362.77) and 12 immature plants, unless a physician’s recommendation documents a different amount needed. Limits in the adult-use and medicinal subsections may not be combined to exceed either lane’s caps.

Possession limits outside the store track similar distinctions. Adults 21 and older may possess up to 28.5 grams of cannabis that is not concentrated and up to 8 grams of concentrate under Health and Safety Code section 11362.1, subject to other restrictions. Medicinal patients have a separate possession framework under the Medical Marijuana Program statutes, commonly summarized as up to eight ounces of dried cannabis absent a recommendation for more. Shoppers should not treat a store’s willingness to sell a legal amount as permission to leave an open package on the front seat or to smoke in a park.

What usually does not change at the counter is San Jose’s local scarcity and registration rule. Whether you are adult-use or medical, the retailer should still appear on SJPD’s registered list. Delivery still requires city authorization. Taxes still stack. Public consumption remains illegal in public places under state Health and Safety Code section 11362.3, with limited exceptions that do not turn downtown sidewalks into lounges. Hotels and landlords may ban on-site use regardless of medical status.

Medical patients sometimes ask whether a recommendation unlocks lower taxes or federal employment protection. California’s compassionate-use donation pathway under SB 34 is a separate program for donated medicinal product, not a universal price cut for every medical purchase. Federal employment, firearm, and immigration rules remain independent of a city retail visit. Schedule III federal medical developments discussed elsewhere in this paper are not a license to ignore ID rules at a San Jose cash wrap.

A practical approach for first-timers: decide whether you are shopping adult-use or medical before you arrive; bring the matching documents; ask the budtender which daily limits apply to your lane if you are buying multiple product types; and keep purchases sealed for the trip home. The counter’s job is to verify eligibility and complete a lawful sale. Your job is to know which eligibility story you are telling.

Local adults who already shop licensed stores know the pattern: verify the address, read the label, and ignore anything that sounds too informal to be legal. That habit matters as much on a quiet Tuesday as it does during a busy weekend promotion calendar.

For everyday San Jose shoppers, the through-line is verification over rumor: confirm the retailer on SJPD’s registered list, read the California label before you buy, and treat tax lines as part of the legal market rather than a personal slight from staff. That habit keeps scarce storefronts accountable and keeps informal sellers from winning on convenience alone.
